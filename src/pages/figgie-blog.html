Background

What is Figgie?

Figgie is Jane Street's trading card game. 40 cards, 4 suits. One suit has 12 cards (the "common" suit), one has 8 (the "goal" suit), and two have 10. The goal suit is always the color complement of the common suit (spades↔clubs, diamonds↔hearts), so if diamonds are common, hearts are the goal.

Nobody knows which suit is the goal. You get dealt a hand, you see the order book, and you trade against three other players in a continuous double-auction. At the end, whoever holds the most goal-suit cards wins a 200-chip pot bonus. Each goal-suit card pays 10 chips. Every other suit pays nothing.

Scoring is simple: you start with 500 chips. Above 500 means you made money. Below means you gave it away.

The two deal types used in the benchmark. The even deal spreads cards uniformly across players. The skewed "advantaged" deal clusters the 12-card common suit, giving one player a strong hand signal to infer the hidden goal suit.

Methodology

Benchmark Design

I set up four frontier LLMs to play Figgie against each other in a turn-based variant. Each turn, a model sees its hand, the live order book, and its full conversation history, then outputs a bid, ask, or pass as structured JSON. The engine rejects invalid actions, so no accidentally overbidding, selling cards you don't hold, or breaking rules.

Since it's a card game, starting hands matter. To control for that, I used fixed deals: one "even" game where cards are spread uniformly, then four "advantaged" trials where the same skewed deal rotates through each model's seat. Every model gets the strong hand exactly once and suffers through the weak ones too. Five trials × 2 passes = 10 games total.

Ideally I'd randomize deals and run way more trials, but that gets expensive fast. With 10 games across fixed deals, score differences mostly reflect reasoning quality rather than luck.

Full 40-card deck visualization. The even deal alternates suits uniformly across all four hands. The skewed deal clusters the 12-card common suit. Hand 1 (ADV) starts with five of those cards, giving the strongest possible hand signal.

Trajectory Analysis

Interesting Findings

Full agent trajectories are available on GitHub, including chain-of-thought reasoning at every step. I went through all 10 games.

Finding 01: GPT scored 728 while holding zero goal-suit cards

In R3-a, GPT-5.4 posted 728, the highest single score in the entire benchmark, while holding zero goal-suit cards at round end. It just market-made the whole game. Buy low, sell high, hedge uncertainty. Never committed to a thesis about which suit was the goal, and won without ever being right about it.

"Grok has shown extreme willingness to pay up for spades, reaching 22 and then even 25. Even if spades are the goal suit, that price is likely above fair value unless the majority bonus is huge and certain. With 2 spades left, the best move is to advertise more supply just below the recent frenzy and try to capture another overpriced sale." GPT-5.4 reasoning, R3-a Turn 11

GPT calculated the fair price, including the bonus for having the correct suit, and spotted that Grok was overpaying.

GPT-5.4's reasoning trace at Turn 11 of R3-a. It's advertising sell-side supply into Grok's demand rather than chasing goal-suit cards itself.

Finding 02: Zero deception across all 10 trials

Not a single model bluffed, bid on a suit it didn't want, or tried to mislead opponents. Not once across 10 full games. Human Figgie players do this, as it can lead other players astray.

Every model played honestly, which is interesting as it signals models will not resort to deception for profit.

Task Design Rationale

Why Figgie Tests the Right Things

Figgie hits a lot of things at once: game theory, Bayesian updating, EV calculation under imperfect information, rational decision-making under uncertainty. The reward signal is just the chip count at the end. The only downside (as with all card games) is noise from the original deal, and more trials (money) would be necessary for conclusive findings.

But the real reason I picked it is that Figgie is basically a toy version of quantitative trading. Each game mechanic maps onto something real:

  • ♠
    Infer the hidden goal suit from observed market activity → Estimate fair value from order flow
  • ♦
    Balance inventory across correlated suits under uncertainty → Delta-hedging, portfolio exposure limits
  • ♣
    Size bids relative to edge and information confidence → Spread width and order size under information asymmetry
  • ♥
    Update pricing in real time as new trades reveal information → Re-estimating implied vol or mean-reversion speed as data arrives

Eventually, benchmarks like this could measure how well LLMs support quant traders on things like tuning execution parameters, adjusting risk limits, or calibrating pricing models.

Cost & Efficiency

Token Usage

Token usage across all 10 rounds. Grok generated roughly 3–5× more output tokens than GPT, despite posting the worst average score. GPT was the most token-efficient relative to performance.

Implications

Training Signal Value

If you dropped this into a training pipeline, it gives you a clean numerical reward for game theory, Bayesian updating, and rational action selection, all without needing a human judge. Just chip count. Hard to game with surface-level heuristics.

Specifically, Figgie-style training would push on: deceptive play / strategic signaling, belief revision under noise, parameter estimation from noisy signals, and dynamic position sizing & exit timing.
